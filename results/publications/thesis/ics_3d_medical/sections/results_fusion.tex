\subsection{Image--Label Fusion}\label{sec:results-fusion}

Two comparisons follow, each isolating one design choice. The first asks
whether moving from Medverse's dual U-Net and cross-attention to a shared
encoder with attention already helps, before anything else about this
thesis's architecture is considered; the second asks whether keeping image
and label evidence as separate streams -- dual attention -- helps further,
within that same encoder-and-attention family.

\paragraph{Setup.} \autoref{tab:relatedwork} classifies Medverse's fusion strategy as early
concatenation: image and label channels are merged before a shared U-Net processes them,
committing to a single fusion point at the very start of the network. This arm is labeled
\textbf{Early Concat} throughout, not Medverse: it starts from Medverse's released weights
(the best available starting point for this architecture) but is then trained under the
identical recipe as the other two arms for the same $\sim$250 epochs -- a different thing
from the released, unmodified baseline evaluated elsewhere in this thesis
(\autoref{sec:results-cascade}). The single-axis
control isolates the architectural family this comparison targets: it keeps the shared
encoder and attention stack common to every architecture in this thesis, but collapses
image and label evidence into one token per cell via a pixel shuffle--unshuffle operation
\emph{before} the transformer -- echoing Iris's own fusion mechanism in spirit though not
in implementation, and withholding dual attention until the next step of the staircase.

Its transformer depth is raised from $l{=}4$ to $l{=}9$ specifically to compute-match the
dual-attention arm (removing the modality axis also removes a large share of the model's
compute; parameter count and compute could not be matched simultaneously, leaving
parameters 52\% higher for the single-axis control). This adjustment is made relative to
dual attention only, not to Early Concat, so the Early Concat comparison alone is not
compute-matched.

All three arms train under an identical recipe (\autoref{tab:fusion-trainsettings}), an
identical data pipeline, and an identical augmentation recipe, but not an identical
starting point: Early Concat keeps its released initialization, while the single-axis and
dual-attention arms warm-start only the encoder and decoder from a shared checkpoint, with
the entire in-context reasoning core (transformer, image/label embeddings, context/query
id, register tokens, cascade projection, pool projection) randomly initialized for both --
so neither patchset arm starts from an already-trained transformer. Training uses real
anatomy only ($p_\text{synth}{=}0$), CT only. The three arms also did not train to a
matched stopping point: Early Concat trained to full convergence, the patchset arms did not.

All three arms are then evaluated with an identical protocol -- fixed 3\,mm single-level
crops, fp32, each model's own best checkpoint -- across every dataset this thesis
evaluates (\autoref{sec:results-setup}), not TotalSegmentator alone.

Rows below are grouped by each dataset's relationship to the training vocabulary, not by
how different the source otherwise looks: TotalSegmentator itself (61 seen / 56 unseen
classes) and FLARE22 (11 seen / 2 unseen, out of 13 total, \autoref{sec:results-setup}) both
reuse the training vocabulary and so split into a genuine seen/unseen pair; MSD Prostate and
MSD Hippocampus introduce organs with no vocabulary overlap at all, so every class in that
pair is unseen by construction, not by a train/val split; HU\_LWK1's target is a
HU-measurement region, not a segmentable anatomical object, a categorically different kind
of ``unseen'' from a novel organ; TotalSegmentator's own MRI acquisition isolates a held-out
\emph{modality} instead, over classes the vocabulary already contains.

\paragraph{Results.} Single-axis alone already beats Early Concat on five of the eight rows --
both TotalSegmentator splits, both FLARE22 splits, and TotalSeg MRI -- while Early Concat wins
the three remaining rows: MSD Prostate, MSD Hippocampus, and HU\_LWK1. Restoring dual
attention flips one of those three (MSD Hippocampus, 0.184 vs.\ 0.172) without touching the
other two, bringing the full architecture to six wins out of eight against Early Concat. The
one aggregate number in the table -- mean of the five held-out sources -- shows why that
flip matters: single-axis alone is a net regression against Early Concat there (0.264 vs.\
0.278), and only once dual attention restores the modality axis does the full architecture
overtake Early Concat (0.297). The architectural family change alone, in other words, is not
what carries this result; dual attention is. The compute picture is mixed rather than
uniformly favorable: dual attention has the fewest parameters and the lowest latency of the
three, but not the fewest GFLOPs -- Early Concat remains cheapest by that measure despite its
lower accuracy, so no arm here is a strict win on both accuracy and compute at once. Two
caveats keep this from a single-variable ablation: Early Concat starts from a pretrained
initialization rather than the randomly-initialized reasoning core the patchset arms share,
and does not receive the same query-conditioning signal during training -- so this is best
read as a comparison against Early Concat's strongest available starting point, not a fully
symmetric ablation. \todo{Re-read once Early Concat receives the same query-conditioning
signal -- currently dropped due to a training instability under compilation.}

\paragraph{Status.} Neither patchset arm had reached its full training budget at the point
these numbers were read, while Early Concat had already converged -- so
\autoref{tab:fusion-staircase} may understate Early Concat's eventual ceiling more than either
patchset arm's. All three arms are N=1 seed, and several rows rest on very few classes
(FLARE22's unseen split, both MSD sources, HU\_LWK1) -- directional evidence, not settled
effect sizes. \todo{Re-read once all three arms reach their full training budget; add
